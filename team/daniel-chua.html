Co-founder & Director

Daniel Chua

He builds AI tools inside a company and trains the staff to use them. That is more or less this job.

WhatsApp us →

Daniel is a co-founder and director of Blueprint AI Training. Like Jian Han, he teaches the courses himself. The company was set up so that the people who design the material are the people who deliver it.

Before Blueprint

Daniel is an AI developer at a crypto company, where he builds AI systems for internal use: a data agent, an SQL agent, an automated reporting system. Building them turned out to be half the job. The other half is getting colleagues to actually use them, which is the same problem Blueprint exists to solve, only inside one company instead of many.

He has also run his own digital marketing agency since 2019, covering web design, SEO and SEM, Facebook ads, content, personal branding, and more recently software and AI development. One postnatal confinement centre returned RM500,000 on RM8,000 of ad spend in six months. He has since rebuilt that team around Claude and other AI tools, so when he tells a room this changes how the work gets done, he is describing something he has already been through as an employer.

The years in between were spent between product and marketing. At MoneyLion he worked as a data analyst, building machine learning models aimed at revenue, customer lifetime value, retention and product sign-up rate. At CoinGecko he was a growth marketer, where the main effort went into the company's presence on YouTube and the channel passed 100,000 subscribers.

What he teaches

Think first, then AI.

That line is his, and it is the thing he repeats most often in a room. The point is not that AI is useless. It is that AI is fast, and fast is not the same as right. It hands you the quickest answer, which is often not the best one, and it gets things wrong more than most people assume. So think the problem through yourself first, and bring AI to it second. Trust your own reasoning, and treat the machine as something to check rather than something to obey.

He teaches on the 2 day AI-Ready Workshop, the course most companies start with. It is conducted with Claude, and the aim is comfort rather than coverage.

Most of his attention in a session goes to the people who did not particularly want to be there. Every group has a few, and they are often the ones whose work would change the most. The material is written for IT and non-IT backgrounds alike, no coding is required at any point, and everyone leaves having applied AI to a real task from their own job.

What he writes about

He writes about avoidance. Nobody announces that they are steering clear of AI, so it shows up indirectly: work that takes exactly as long as it always did, proposals still being written on a Sunday night. His argument is that avoiding AI is a perfectly rational choice for an employee, right up until leadership makes it safe not to.
