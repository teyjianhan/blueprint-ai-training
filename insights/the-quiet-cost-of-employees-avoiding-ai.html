The quiet cost of employees who avoid AI

Daniel Chua · Co-founder & Director 8 July 2026 4 min read

Nobody in your company is going to tell you they're avoiding AI. It doesn't come up in meetings. It doesn't appear in any report. There is no line item for it.

Here's what it looks like instead. A manager spends Sunday night on a proposal that could have started from a solid draft in twenty minutes. An executive reads a forty page tender line by line because "that's how we've always checked them." A marketing exec types nothing into an AI assistant at work because a colleague once said the company "might not allow it," and no one ever clarified whether that was true.

Each of these is small. None of them will show up this quarter. That's exactly what makes the cost dangerous. It's slow drift rather than a dramatic failure, and companies rarely notice drift until a competitor makes it visible.

Avoidance is rational. That's the problem.

It's tempting to read all this as staff being stubborn or old-fashioned. From where I sit, running training rooms, the picture is different. The avoidance usually makes perfect sense from the employee's side.

Some are worried about looking replaceable. If AI can draft my report, why does the company need me? Some tried a tool once, got a mediocre answer, and reasonably concluded it wasn't worth their time. Nobody showed them the difference between a lazy prompt and a proper one. And many simply don't know what's allowed. When there's no policy and no signal from above, the safest career move is to not touch the thing.

If nobody senior has said "we want you to use this, and here's how to do it safely," then not using it is the correct decision. Your people aren't behind. They're waiting.

I want to be fair to the employees here, because the usual framing blames them. In most companies I visit, the staff are not resisting change. They are responding accurately to silence.

What the comfortable version looks like

The contrast, once you've seen it, is hard to unsee. In a team that's genuinely comfortable with AI, the tools get opened the way email gets opened, without ceremony and without glancing at the door. People know which tasks to hand over, which to keep, and how to check what comes back. The quiet Sunday night work shrinks. The forty page tender gets summarised first and verified after, and the verification is where the human effort goes.

None of that comes from a subscription. It comes from permission, a few clear safety rules, and hands-on practice on the person's own work. Enough wins in their own job, and the fear loses its grip.

Comfort is trainable

This is the part I find genuinely optimistic. Fear of AI isn't a personality trait. It's a gap in experience, and gaps in experience close quickly under the right conditions. Two focused days is usually enough to move a room from wary to fluent. Not expert, just fluent. Enough that on Wednesday morning, back at their desks, they reach for the tool instead of around it.

But the first move belongs to leadership. Someone senior has to say, out loud: we want you using this, here's the training, here are the rules, and nobody is being replaced for getting good at it. Until that sentence is said, the avoidance continues, politely and invisibly, at a cost you'll only be able to measure once it's already large.
