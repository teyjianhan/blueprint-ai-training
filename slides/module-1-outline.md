## Slide 3 — Section: What is AI? · Theory

*Drafted directly in Canva as three bare questions with no answers; content below finalized on 2026-08-26.*

A basics primer ahead of slide 4 — establishes the vocabulary (AI vs. the narrower "Gen AI") before the business-productivity framing starts.

**What is AI?** Any system that performs tasks normally requiring human intelligence — recognizing patterns, making decisions, understanding language.

**How many types of AI are there?** Three tiers, by capability/autonomy — not by what a system can do, but by how self-directed and unbounded its reasoning is:
- *Narrow AI* — built for specific tasks, from a one-job system like a spam filter to something as broad and flexible as Claude. However capable it gets at "many things," it's still not general — no self-directed goals, no continuous learning from experience, no reasoning across truly unlimited domains.
- *General AI (AGI)* — hypothetical, doesn't exist yet: human-equivalent, self-directed reasoning and learning across any domain.
- *Super AI (ASI)* — hypothetical, beyond General AI.

**What is Gen AI?** The subset of *Narrow* AI that creates new content — text, images, code, analysis — instead of just classifying or predicting. Claude is generative AI: broad and flexible, but still Narrow AI in the formal sense, not AGI.

(Deliberately corrected mid-draft: generative AI feels general because it's so flexible, but it classifies as Narrow AI — a useful, slightly counterintuitive fact for a business audience, and it quietly reinforces slide 8's "AI can never replace humans" line rather than undercutting it.)

## Slide 4 — Section: The role of generative AI in business · Theory

*Deck has an AI / Business / Data three-circle diagram with no copy yet; content below finalized on 2026-08-26, read as a flow rather than a Venn overlap — revisit if the diagram itself was actually meant to show overlap.*

**Data** — the raw material: numbers, documents, customer records, conversations the business already generates every day.

**AI** — the engine that turns that raw data into something usable — patterns, drafts, analysis — at a speed no team matches by hand.

**Business** — where it lands: faster decisions, freed-up time, and a productivity edge for whoever adopts it first.

Closing line: generative AI's role in business isn't replacing judgment, it's compressing the distance between data and decision.

## Slide 5 — Section: Is using AI at work "cheating"? · Theory

Not in the source course outline — added to name the quiet objection some participants walk in with, before it becomes a distraction later.

**The frame:** a bad manager dumps a task on a junior with no context, no check-in and no review, then blames the junior when it comes back wrong. A good manager briefs properly, discusses the approach, and reviews the work together — the junior grows, and so does the output. Using AI works the same way. Handing AI a vague ask and shipping whatever comes back unreviewed is the bad-manager pattern; briefing it properly and checking its work (modules 2 and 9) is the good-manager pattern.

**The reframe:** the question isn't "is AI allowed" — it's "am I managing it well." Delegation was never cheating when done with a junior; it isn't cheating done with AI either, as long as the same judgement gets applied.

## Slide 6 — Interactive: is your team already judging AI use? · Practical

*Drafted directly in Canva, not previously in this outline — the live payoff of slide 5's reframe, run as a Kahoot-driven bit rather than a straight lecture.*

Opens with a show-of-hands prompt: "Raise your hand if you don't like your employees using AI for work." Then a run of gut-check questions — does it bother you if an employee uses AI to write emails? to do all of their work? — put to a live Kahoot round. A second Kahoot round follows on the opposite case: how do you feel about an employee who has never used, and never will use, AI for their work?

The point lands either way the room votes: discomfort with AI-assisted work and complacency about AI-avoidant work are the same double standard slide 5 just named — this makes participants feel it via their own answers instead of just hearing the argument.

## Slide 9 — Section: Models, tokens and context · Theory

*Content finalized on 2026-08-26, built around a single analogy: model as a person you hire.*

Headline: Think of Claude like hiring a person, not buying software.

**Model = the person you hire.** Different models are like different hires — junior and fast versus senior and thorough. You pick the right person for the job, not the same one for everything (sets up slide 10 directly).

**Tokens = their brainpower spent on the task.** Every word they read and every word they write draws on it — a longer brief and a longer answer take more effort, the same way a longer report takes a person more time and energy. That's why usage and cost scale with length.

**Context = the domain knowledge you've briefed them with, this sitting.** Not what they permanently know — everything you've told them so far in this conversation: the brief, the documents, the back-and-forth. Once that briefing gets too long or too sprawling, they start losing track of what you said at the start — that's the cue to bring in someone fresh rather than keep piling on. (Light foreshadow only — the full "context rot" mechanic is taught properly in Module 2's slide 3, so this doesn't need to spell it out.)

Speaker note: the person-analogy is the throughline for the whole slide, not a one-off comparison — it's the same frame slide 10 picks back up ("now you know what your hire's brainpower and briefing capacity look like — here's how to choose the right hire for the job").

## Slide 11 — Section: The Claude 6 Main Pillars · Theory

*Content finalized on 2026-08-26. Kept as a single summary slide for now rather than splitting into six — still flagged as the strongest candidate to expand into a mini-sequence if the room has time; easy to split later since each pillar already has its own line.*

Headline: The Claude 6 Main Pillars — the frame for the next two days.

1. **Read** — pull the substance out of documents, data and images without doing it line by line yourself. (Comes up throughout; goes deepest in Module 6's data analysis.)
2. **Write** — draft anything from a routine email to a full strategic recommendation, briefed properly. (Module 2.)
3. **Analyse** — turn raw numbers into diagnosis and a recommendation, not just a summary. (Module 6.)
4. **Automate** — turn something you'd otherwise repeat by hand into a procedure Claude just runs. (Module 4's Skills, Module 10's no-code builds.)
5. **Think** — use Claude as a reasoning partner that pushes back, not just a yes-man. (Module 5.)
6. **Build** — go from a conversation to a working output: a dashboard, a tool, an app. (Module 9's Artifacts, Module 10's no-code builds.)

Closing line: everything over the next two days is one of these six pillars in practice — this slide is the map, the hands-on modules are where you actually walk it.
